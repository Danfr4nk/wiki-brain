# Fall Out Boy

**The short version.** Fall Out Boy is not just another band on a favorites list for Dan Frank. They are a time capsule of his teenage years — a band he chased across state lines in 2007, saw play a secret show under a fake name, and watched premiere an album on live television. The affection is real but frozen in time. He loves the band they were from 2001 through 2008, and he has effectively disowned everything they released after their 2013 reunion. The love stops at the album *Folie à Deux* and does not move forward.

## The band

Fall Out Boy formed in Chicago in 2001 and became the commercial flagship of the mid-2000s emo-adjacent pop-punk scene, alongside My Chemical Romance and Panic! at the Disco. Bassist Pete Wentz wrote the lyrics — dense, self-aware, footnote-heavy. Frontman Patrick Stump's melodic instincts turned those lyric sheets into radio hits without sanding off the density.

## The touring year (2007)

Dan did not just listen to Fall Out Boy. He followed them on the ground. In roughly eleven months during 2007, he saw the band three times and attended a fourth televised event — a record he kept in a Facebook "Concerts I've gone to" note that survives in the archive.

| **Date** | **What** | **Where** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| January 11, 2007 | Fall Out Boy | Agora, Cleveland |
| February 6, 2007 | Fall Out Boy — TRL Studios taping | New York City |
| November 19, 2007 | Fall Out Boy secret show (billed as "Schrute Farms") | Mohawk Place, Buffalo |
| May 6, 2007 | Say Anything (same touring circuit) | Rams Head, Baltimore |

The Facebook posts from that year back this up. Dan wrote things like "swooning over the new FOB" and "in New York City to see Fall Out Boy at TRL." This was not passive fandom. A teenager in southwest Pennsylvania routing himself to Cleveland, New York City, and Buffalo to see the same band inside a single year is the opposite of someone who simply has a band on a playlist.

The February 6, 2007, TRL appearance was the rollout gimmick for the album *Infinity on High*. The band taped the *Total Request Live* broadcast on the album's release day as part of a three-city, three-show release-week stunt. Dan was in the studio for it. He was still engaging with that record twelve years later, texting in 2019: "i'm listening to infinity on high and its like a really good album." Notice the title he reaches for is the 2007 album, not anything the band released after their hiatus.

## The Buffalo secret show

The November 19, 2007, Buffalo date was a secret show. Fall Out Boy played under the alias "Schrute Farms" — a reference to the television show *The Office*. That tells you something about the kind of in-joke Dan was operating in at seventeen. Decades later, he still treated the night as a genuine anomaly worth verifying. On July 22, 2025, he texted: "chatgpt just informed me that the secret fall out boy show i went to is the first and ONLY time that [Take This To Your Grave] was played in its entirety."

Whether or not ChatGPT was right, the instinct is revealing: treating a 2007 basement show as a unique historical data point worth running through an artificial intelligence. He was there. It was the only full play-through of their debut album ever. He logged it.

## Where the love cuts off

The affection has a hard edge. A 2019 message states it plainly: "I can't be a hater because I love most of the albums but only folie a deux and prior." *Folie à Deux* (2008) was the band's last record before their 2009 hiatus. Everything after the 2013 reunion — the album *Save Rock and Roll* and onward — is absent from his favorites, his concert history, and his retrospective commentary.

By 2025, the cutoff had hardened into a joke of its own. In one text listing hypothetical groomsmen, he wrote "a bag of cocaine, the first 3 fall out boy albums and the ghost of gore vidal" (May 5, 2025). A separate message from February 17, 2025, reads: "And more frightening is that I'm listening to old fall out boy and am like 'wow I really get it now.'" Old, not new. The band he loves is the 2001–2008 band. The post-hiatus band is, for his purposes, a different act he has chosen not to claim.

## The deep cuts, not the hits

In his manually curated "MUSIC LIST (start-2024)" — not a passive Spotify like-feed but a deliberate selection — Dan logged six Fall Out Boy tracks. None of them is the band's actual hit. There is no "Sugar, We're Goin Down." There is no "Dance, Dance." What is here instead:

- "Tell That Mick He Just Made My List of Things to Do Today"

- "Yule Shoot Your Eye Out" (a Christmas EP original)

- "Thriller" (a Michael Jackson cover from that same Christmas release)

- "Our Lawyer Made Us Change The Name Of The Song So We Wouldn't Get Sued"

- "What A Catch, Donnie"

This is a completist's list, not a fan's. It is the kind of selection someone makes after having already absorbed the singles years earlier and moved on to the material that only a listener who cared about the whole band would bother with. Two of the six songs are built around bits — the lawyer-forced-a-rename premise, the "Tell That Mick" non-sequitur. That title-as-joke habit is Fall Out Boy's own signature: burying real sentiment inside a title that is doing standup first.

"What A Catch, Donnie" is the one worth stopping on. It is a *Folie à Deux* track built as a mock eulogy for Patrick Stump. A dozen guest vocalists from the peer scene — Panic! at the Disco, Cobra Starship, Gym Class Heroes, and others — sing tributes to a man who is not dead, framed as if the band itself is already a legacy act being memorialized. It is a song about a band writing its own eulogy in advance, sincerely, while still very much alive. Choosing that song, specifically, out of an entire discography reads as recognition, not coincidence.

One important caveat: Dan does not process sung lyrics as language while listening. Words register as sound texture, not semantic content. So the reading above — built around specific lyrical and thematic content — likely reflects contextual knowledge of what these songs are about rather than in-the-moment lyric comprehension driving the attraction. Treat the track-selection pattern (deep cuts over hits, whole-catalog engagement) as the solid evidence. Treat the "why" as speculative.

## What would prove this reading wrong

1. **A post-2013 Fall Out Boy track appearing in his favorites.** Any logged affection for *Save Rock and Roll* or later material would break the frozen-at-*Folie* claim.

1. **A concert attendance after 2008.** The record shows no Fall Out Boy attendance after the hiatus. A documented post-reunion show would challenge the disavowal.

1. **A hit single appearing in the deep-cut list.** If "Sugar, We're Goin Down" or "Dance, Dance" surfaced in his favorites, the completist-over-hits reading would need revision.

## Predictions

1. **The frozen cutoff will hold.** Dan will continue to reach for *Infinity on High* and *Folie à Deux* and ignore the post-reunion catalog. The band he loves is the band he grew up with, and that is not going to change.

1. **The Buffalo secret show will keep its status as a signature story.** It combines fandom, secrecy, a television reference, and a unique historical claim. It is too good a story to let go.

1. **Any future Fall Out Boy listening will be framed as nostalgia, not discovery.** He will talk about "old Fall Out Boy" with the warmth of something rediscovered, never as a new fan of the current band.

## What we still don't know

- **The exact mechanism of the freeze.** Whether the cutoff is about the music itself, the identity stakes of the 2007 era, or some combination is not directly stated anywhere in the record.

- **Whether he has heard the post-hiatus albums at all.** Absence from the favorites list does not necessarily mean absence from his ears. He may have listened and simply not logged it.

- **The role of the peer scene.** Fall Out Boy's guest-vocalist network overlaps heavily with the other bands in his cluster (My Chemical Romance, Say Anything, Taking Back Sunday). Whether the attraction is to the band specifically or to the whole scene ecosystem is difficult to separate.
