# Eclecticism in Favorites

**The short version.** Dan keeps a list of 2,016 things he likes — books, art, films, music. For a long time the obvious reading was that he simply has broad taste. That reading is wrong. The list is really two different habits sharing one spreadsheet. With books, art and film he picks one subject, collects account after account of it until he has covered it, and then stops. With music he keeps three groups of artists running side by side for eighteen years, because he is using them, not finishing them. Both habits leave the same fingerprint — lots of names, almost none repeated — so from a distance they look like one wandering curiosity. They are two opposite operations.

## What the list looked like, and why that was misleading

The numbers that made "broad taste" seem obvious are real:

- **120 books by 98 different authors.** 85 of those authors appear exactly once — 86.7% of them.

- **25 artworks by 25 different makers.** Not one repeat. A 100% singleton rate.

- **1,860 music tracks by 1,477 artists.** That is about 1.26 tracks per artist, and 86.6% of the artists show up once.

Almost no repeats anywhere. That looks like somebody sampling everything and settling on nothing. But counting *creators* was the wrong thing to count.

Count subjects instead, and the book shelf changes shape completely. 40 of the 120 books are about Trump or January 6th, written by 30 authors. Another 20 are about the Roman republic, Caesar, or ancient history, written by 14 authors. Those two sets share no authors at all. So **half the shelf is two subjects, read through 44 different hands.**

That is not a person with wide-ranging literary taste. That is a person investigating two events and getting as many independent witnesses as he can. Most authors appear once because one account per witness is all you need.

The art is the cleanest example, because it is small enough to check in full. 25 works, 25 makers, no repeats — the maximum possible score for "eclectic." But 24 of the 25 carry the same handful of subject tags: wound, observer, collapse, glitch, rupture, fortress. Six subjects, twenty-five hands. The reason no artist repeats is that in art, a single painting is the unit — not an artist's whole body of work. So the same collecting habit that makes books look varied makes art look *completely* varied, purely as an artifact of how art is counted.

## System A: collect until you hit the edge

Here is the first habit, stated plainly:

Pick something with a findable edge. Gather independent accounts of it until you reach that edge. Then stop.

Rome stops at Augustus. There is an end to the story, he gets there, and he is done. The Trump and January 6th shelf will either stop when the documentary record is covered, or it will keep adding witnesses to the same single event — either way, it is one object being surrounded, not a genre being browsed.

The short end of the book distribution supports this too. Woodward 5, Wolff 5, Goldsworthy 4, Karl 3 is not a list of favorite writers. It is the handful of people who wrote more than one useful account of the two events in question.

Film probably works the same way, but the record is too thin to score it. There are 11 titles listed with no directors, no tags and no dates. One clue: three of eleven canonical films elsewhere in his record are Kubrick, and in 2017 he wrote *"theres only one kubrick movie i haven't seen ... and eyes wide shut."* That is a man reporting his position inside a set he is finishing. Eyes Wide Shut is on the favorites list. It is a hint, not proof.

## System B: three toolkits, all running

Music is 92.3% of the whole list by volume, and it does not behave like the books at all.

The giveaway is not the artist column — that looks identical to the books. The giveaway is **time**. 872 of the 1,860 tracks, 47%, were released in 2024 or later. Of the 1,396 tracks that carry a release date, 62% are from the last two years. A set you are trying to finish does not keep admitting new members at that rate. "Music released recently" has no edge to reach.

The library also arrived in two distinct waves, doing two different jobs:

- A hand-made list of **463 tracks** called "MUSIC LIST (start-2024)". It holds the entire emo and pop-punk collection — Fall Out Boy, My Chemical Romance, Say Anything, Taking Back Sunday, New Found Glory. Somebody sat down and deliberately archived a decade-old attachment.

- A live Spotify feed of **1,384 tracks** from 2025 and 2026. It holds nearly all of the electronic and bass music — LYNY, Knock2, Effin, Mau P, PEEKABOO, Levity.

Inside those waves are three groups, each about 5% of the library, none of them dominant, and each doing a specific job:

| **The group** | **What it is for** | **How we know it is not just "a favorite genre"** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emo and pop-punk | A frozen archive of who he was, roughly 2005–2010 | His Fall Out Boy interest stops dead at the 2008 album *Folie à Deux* and does not follow the band's later reunion. The 2024 list is a deliberate re-filing, not a new discovery. |
| Electronic and bass | Reference listening for his own music production | It is the live 2025–26 feed. It lines up with his own bass-heavy production work restarting. He is scouting. |
| Experimental hip-hop | Confrontational, dense, close to what he makes | JPEGMAFIA is the closest match to his own production identity. Current, not frozen. |

That is the difference. He is not a listener in music — he is a **producer**. He has released under four names since around 2013, all of them bass-heavy. When you produce in a field, your intake never finishes, because scouting has no edge. You keep the tools you are still using.

**And these three groups are at least eighteen years old.** This is the fact that kills the idea that any of it is a recent Spotify discovery. His Facebook record has Fall Out Boy and Say Anything named in 2007 statuses — *"currently swooning over the new FOB,"* *"in love with the new Say Anything disc"* — with Radiohead on loop the same year. Elliott Smith was liked in August 2013, and there are 7 Elliott Smith tracks in the library today. The electronic side has the same long tail: likes for ODESZA, JAUZ, Borgore, Flying Lotus, Diplo and Zedd between 2012 and 2014, plus Electric Zoo in 2010 and a run of raves and DJ shows from 2014 to 2019.

The recent surge in *volume* is real — 530 tracks from 2025 and 197 from 2026 — and it lines up with a hard period in his life and his return to Pennsylvania. But volume going up is a person switching the music back on. The groups themselves are not new.

One caution the entry insists on: **lyrics are not the mechanism here.** Dan has said that sung words register to him as texture, not as meaning, while he is listening. So any claim that he likes the emo records for their themes of betrayal or self-blame is either background knowledge about those albums or a guess about how a voice sounds. This system is about sound and function, not about reading.

## Why the two systems look identical from outside

Both habits produce a long list of names that almost never repeat.

System A does it because one witness per source is enough to triangulate an event. System B does it because a producer sampling current bass music, or re-filing an emo catalogue at one or two tracks per artist, has no use for artist loyalty either.

There is a standard statistic for how concentrated a collection is. Run it on the favorites and you get 0.188 for music, 0.166 for books, and 0.000 for art — all very flat, all apparently the same. So those figures are **not measuring a personality.** They are the arithmetic of two different sampling rules that happen to share one habit: don't repeat a creator unless the creator *is* the subject.

It is worth seeing how unusual that flatness is for this particular person. Run the same statistic on who he actually talks to — 105,405 messages across 496 contacts — and it comes out at 0.9601, which is close to a monopoly. The same man has a near-total concentration in his social life and a near-flat one in his taste. That contrast is real and it is not what this entry is disputing. What this entry adds is that the flat half is itself two things.

**The test that separates them is not the creator column. It is whether the category ever stops.** Rome stops. The Trump shelf will stop, or it will keep circling one object. Music does not stop: 2025 added 530 tracks, 2026 added 197 before this entry was rewritten. A set of tools you use to calibrate a live practice cannot close until the practice closes.

## Where this comes from in how his mind works

The older version of this entry explained the list as "high openness" — the personality-test idea of a person drawn to novelty and variety.

That explanation does not survive contact with the actual test results. There is no single openness score in his profile at all. What the profile does report is Artistic Interests at 81 — the one aesthetic measure independently confirmed against his own writing, where the language showed up at 3.82 times the baseline rate. Alongside it: Intellect 95, Liberalism 91, Introspection 87. And one facet called Inquisitive, whose documented pattern is *discovery, immersion, understanding, then abrupt abandonment once the mystery is solved.*

Read that last one again. It is not browsing. It is System A described from the inside — the reason Rome stops at Augustus.

So Artistic Interests 81 explains **that** he curates. It does not explain **how**. The "how" comes from two mental habits that pull in opposite directions:

- One habit closes things. It keeps testing an idea until the idea holds together with nothing left over, and then it is finished with it. That is what produces System A: one more witness, one more account, until the edge.

- The other habit keeps things open. It holds several possibilities live at once and does not need any of them resolved. That is what produces System B: three groups of music running in parallel, indefinitely.

These are not one trait called pattern-seeking. They are two operations that happen to leave the same tracks in a spreadsheet.

A note on where these numbers come from. The personality and cognitive scores were produced by AI assessments Dan commissioned, not by a clinician. This vocabulary shows up 17 times in 106,629 of his outgoing messages, which is to say it lives in those sessions and not in his life. The argument here is built so it does not depend on them: if the scores are wrong, the two systems are still sitting in the spreadsheet, and the Facebook dates and the two waves of music are still what they are.

## What would prove this wrong

The entry commits to four testable things.

1. **A new non-music obsession should look like System A.** Within three months of a named new interest, expect at least 5 independent sources on one subject, and no second work by the first source until the others are used up. **If instead it fans out across many unrelated subjects with one source each, this whole split is wrong** and the old "broad taste" reading was right.

1. **A new music group should be added beside the others, not instead of one.** If a fourth kind of music arrives, hip-hop, emo and bass should all still be there. **The falsifier:** one of the three current groups vanishing from new additions for a year or more while a fourth takes its place, with no change in his production work to explain it.

1. **If listening data ever appears, it should bunch up inside music and stay flat inside books.** A play-count export showing JPEGMAFIA or LYNY taking a disproportionate share of actual listens would confirm the music is being used rather than collected. If music's listening figure came out flat and matched its collection figure of 0.188, then music is being sampled too, and the "being used" claim would have to narrow to production only.

1. **"High openness" should not come back as the explanation.** That idea predicts both systems collapsing into one browsing style. They have not. **The falsifier:** any single category that runs both systems at once — a book shelf that triangulates one event *and* keeps three unrelated subjects going forever, or a music library that finishes a genre and stops.

## What we still don't know

- **There are no play counts anywhere in his records.** This is the biggest missing number in the whole argument. The claim that the music is "being used" currently rests on his production work, the eighteen-year continuity, and what is in the live feed — not on evidence of listening. A single Spotify history export would settle it.

- **The film list is barely recorded.** 11 titles, no directors, no tags, no dates. One pass over the source spreadsheet filtered to films would settle whether films follow System A (collecting a director) or are a third thing entirely.

- **Nobody has checked whether the 2025–26 Spotify tracks were actually chosen by hand.** If most of the electronic music came from algorithmic recommendations rather than deliberate likes, then the "production calibration" reading only applies to the part he picked himself, and the rest is a platform. The spreadsheet has a column that would answer this.

- **There is no record before 2007.** The continuity claim starts at his Facebook registration date. His teenage years in extreme sports look exactly like System A applied to a physical activity — total obsession, scene completed — but that is only attested by him looking back, years later. It would not change the split. It would extend System A's reach beyond the spreadsheet.
