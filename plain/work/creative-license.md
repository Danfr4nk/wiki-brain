# Creative License (NYC, 2011–2012)

**The short version.** Creative License was a small celebrity-talent and music-brokering company in the West Village. Dan worked there for ten months, in 2011 and 2012. The ending took five turns: an airfare complaint, a payroll dispute, a whistleblower disclosure, a notarized act of retaliation, and a threat from LinkedIn. Dan filed a counter-notice. Then he kept his boss's email address for the better part of a decade.

The company president was Kevin McKiernan. He eventually tried to erase Dan's professional identity from LinkedIn, using notarized false statements. Dan fought back. Years later the two were still on each other's contact lists. This is the most fully documented workplace conflict in the whole archive.

## The job itself

Creative License brokered licensed music and celebrity talent. Only one client pitch survives: a Walmart request titled "3rd Party Negotiator — Licensed Music and Celebrity Talent." Earlier guesses had the company doing generic production work. It did not.

Dan's title was **"Special Projects Coordinator."** His résumé later said "Executive Assistant / Office Manager." A LinkedIn notice from 2012, written at the time, uses "Special Projects Coordinator" too. That settles which title is real.

Day to day it was ordinary office work. He covered for migraines and doctor's appointments, which came up again and again — July, September, October and November 2011, then January 2012. He handled client relationships with **Walmart** and **Molson Coors**. He helped prep a colleague for speaking appearances. And once he tracked down a departed employee to recover company property. That last tactic was later turned on him.

In May 2011, the company's Director of Business Affairs, Katherine Palakovich, sent Dan his own non-disclosure and non-compete agreement to sign. Dan forwarded it to his father Rick the same day with a note: "Thought you might want to take a look at this." Rick was consulted on employment paperwork from the very start, not only at the acrimonious end.

## How the ending unfolded, in four acts

**Act zero — the airfare citation.** By Dan's own later account, the reason he gave for quitting was a "small fraud with airplane tickets." Something was off in the billing of Kevin's travel expenses. Dan's job included "oversight of office and employee expenses," so he was placed to notice it.

He is not sure of himself about this in hindsight. He says he used it as "the 'legal ground' to detach." A complaint about someone's personality would have been waved off as him being "sensitive." A "financial anomaly" made the departure "an objective failure" by the company rather than by him.

Years later he still wonders whether he "blew it up into a much much bigger thing," or whether he had simply not yet learned what was normal in the industry.

What the record confirms is only that he cited it. The alleged fraud itself has never been independently checked. The confirmation comes from his own testimony in a later session.

**Act one — the payroll dispute.** Dan's last day was on or before **February 15, 2012** — the date of his first email flagging that his final paycheck had a **$104 charge for a MetroCard he says he never requested or received.** The check also arrived unsigned. Over the next six weeks Dan and the company's accounting officer, Marty Jackson, went back and forth over the missing signature, the disputed charge, and a W-2 that didn't arrive on schedule. Marty's position was that Dan needed to have opted out of the automatic MetroCard program "in writing before you quit," which Dan disputed ever being told. On February 27, after Dan pushed back on Marty's math, Marty wrote the line that anchors this whole episode:

"It's a trust issue. We don't trust you. Please do not forget the keys or any other CL property you have."

Dan escalated. He filed complaints with the **IRS and the NYS Department of Labor**, and copied his father Rick on everything.

Rick acted as a tactical coach, not an authority figure: "You sound so desperate. That is what kevin wants. We'll talk about a strategy for this."

By April 9, Dan closed the dispute directly with Marty. He gave up the $104 to end it. He also drew a line between the accountant and the president: "I didn't mean to give the impression that my hostility was directed at you personally... I don't believe that your intention was to create a volatile or unpleasant situation."

That distinction — between the man carrying out the order and the man giving it — is Dan's own, and he made it at the time.

**Act two — the catharsis.** Two months after leaving, Dan emailed a former coworker, Rachel Rauch. The message was one word: "Enjoy!!!" Attached was a file called `BEST OF KTM VOICEMAILS.mp3` — a compiled recording of McKiernan's "erratic, high-volatility communication style."

The end of the relationship produced an archive, not just a grievance.

**Act three — the whistleblower disclosure.** Months after the payroll dispute ended, Dan wrote to another former contact, Renae Holland. Nobody asked him to. Something had been bothering him.

Back in December 2011, while he still worked there, Kevin had used Dan's own email and LinkedIn account to send recommendation-removal requests to former employees. Renae was one of them. In Dan's words:

"I (my account, rather) was acting as a channel for Kevin to act out his most immature fantasies and I'm glad I can say that I didn't even help write those emails... The more alarming aspect of this was the employment contracts that Kevin had me send to the two former interns. These documents had been altered before they were sent and a clause was added that prohibited recommendations without CEO approval... I think that the two interns should be aware of this for their own legal protection and because it's just disgustingly wrong."

Renae's reply backs him up independently, from her own time at the company: "in my time there I was Kevin falsify more documents that I can count — I know what he is capable of."

This is the substance behind the garbled charge of "altered intern contracts." It names a specific mechanism: a gag clause requiring CEO approval, quietly slipped into departing interns' paperwork. It is not a vague accusation.

**Act four — the retaliation.** A month after the whistleblower email, Kevin filed a formal intellectual-property complaint with LinkedIn against Dan's profile. It was backed by **two notarized documents claiming Dan had never worked at Creative License and had made his job title up.**

Katherine Palakovich notarized them. She used her own notary commission, on behalf of her employer, against a former coworker. Dan called this out to her directly as a breach of professional duty, citing the Notary Public Code.

LinkedIn threatened to close Dan's account within three business days. He filed a counter-notice and offered "paystubs, tax documentation, or any other official documentation." He also named the motive outright: "Mr. McKiernan has and continues to attempt to smear my name because of my actions as a whistleblower while under his employ."

No resolution email was ever found. Whether LinkedIn ruled for Dan is unknown. His profile plainly survived.

## The postscript

After all of that, Dan kept Kevin McKiernan's contact details for close to a decade. Facebook address-book snapshots from 2016 through 2021 still carry `kevin@creativelicense.com`.

And in December 2014, Katherine Palakovich was leaving the company herself. She bcc'd Dan on a mass invitation to an "Ex-Creative License Happy Hour," addressed to "all my favorite ex (and current) CL employees."

So two and a half years after a fight serious enough to pull in the IRS, the NYS Department of Labor and a notarized false statement, Dan was still on the alumni list. Neither fact cancels the other. Both are true.

By February 2012 Dan was also job-hunting in the ordinary way. He was applying to EA and Office Manager roles across Manhattan and contacting firms like the Bond Street Group.

That matters for how much weight the "principled whistleblower exit" reading can take. It contradicts nothing above. But it does mean the departure was probably also a normal job change already under way, rather than a purely dramatic break. That fits Dan's own later doubt about whether the airfare complaint was proportionate.

## What this connects to

The archive names this job and a later one, BFS Foods, as the first and most recent versions of a single shape. They sit fourteen years apart. In both, someone in authority makes a financial claim with no paperwork behind it. In both, Dan answers with an exit and a paper trail rather than a confrontation.

It is also the best-documented case of his distrust of authority, after the BFS one. A company's own accounting officer told him in writing "we don't trust you." Four months later its president backed a false, notarized claim meant to erase Dan's professional identity from LinkedIn. Dan answered with an IRS and Department of Labor complaint and a counter-notice. He did not back down.

## What we still don't know

- **The specific mechanism behind the airfare citation** — what Dan actually found in the travel-expense records, and whether it involved Walmart or Molson Coors billing — is unverified; only the fact that he cited it is confirmed.

- **What specifically happened to Renae Holland** — her phrasing implies she was herself a target of some Kevin McKiernan campaign, but the substance wasn't recovered.

- **The exact text of the notarized documents** Kevin submitted to LinkedIn was not extracted.

- **Whether LinkedIn ultimately ruled for or against Dan** is unconfirmed.
