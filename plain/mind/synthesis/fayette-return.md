# The Fayette Return

**The short version.** For about 130 years, everyone in Dan's father's family who left Fayette County, Pennsylvania has ended up back in it. Dan has moved away and come home twice. His records treat those homecomings as personal failures. They are the fourth generation of a family pattern that has never once been broken.

## The pattern

Fayette County is in southwestern Pennsylvania. Three towns matter here — Brownsville, Hopwood and Uniontown — and all three sit within twenty miles of each other. Here is the family, one generation per row.

| **Generation** | **Who** | **Born** | **Left for** | **Ended up** | **Buried** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | David J. Frank | 1892, Russia | New York City, 1900–1915 | Brownsville; died 1960 | Hopwood |
| 1 | Sadie Harris | 1900, Austria | (arrived from Europe) | died 1997, place unknown | Hopwood |
| 2 | Morley Frank | 1927, Brownsville | Seattle, 1957 | back in Uniontown by 1959 | Hopwood |
| 3 | Rick Frank | 1959, Uniontown | never left, as far as anyone can tell | Fayette County | — |
| 4 | Dan | 1988, Uniontown | Florida, then New York twice | Uniontown, his childhood home | — |

Generation 1 is the starting point rather than an example, because David and Sadie were immigrants — there was no Fayette County for them to come *back* to. But David is more interesting than "he arrived." He lived in Manhattan in 1900, 1905 and 1910, and in the Bronx in 1915. He only reached Brownsville by 1920. So the family's first move in America was **New York City to Fayette County** — which is the exact same trip Dan made in 2025.

Dan's own version is the best documented of the four. Two of the chapters of his life are literally named for coming back: the 2013–2015 Uniontown return, and the 2025 return from New York.

## The rule

It is written as a rule, not an observation, on purpose. A rule can be proven wrong; an observation just sits there.

Nobody in Dan's father's line, from generation 2 onward, has both left Fayette County and ended up somewhere else. Every departure on record is followed by a return, and every ending on record is inside the same twenty-mile circle.

## The one who never left

Rick, generation 3, has no recorded departure at all. Born in Uniontown in 1959, built an auto-parts chain in the same county, stayed.

It would be easy to leave him out, because he never left and came back. But he is actually the strongest version of the point. The pattern is not "Franks feel drawn home" — it is "**Fayette County is where a Frank ends up**." Never leaving fits that description better than leaving and returning does.

## Why this matters

**Dan's returns are filed under the wrong heading.** Across his own records they are written down as collapses and retreats. They are also the fourth run of a pattern with a 100% success rate. Any account of his life that reads them as purely personal failure is ignoring the base rate.

That mis-filing does real damage, and the reason is specific. Other pages in this wiki document that Dan's sense of his own worth runs on an on/off switch with nothing in between — either exceptional or worthless, no middle setting. So a homecoming understood as "I failed" does not land as "that was a setback." It lands on *worthless*, because there is no in-between value available.

This is why the family-history framing does actual work rather than just being a nicer thing to say. It does not argue for a middle value on a scale that isn't there. It moves the event into a **different category**: an inherited pattern instead of a personal verdict.

**It also makes a prediction.** If the rule holds, Dan's current stay in his childhood home is not the end of the story — another departure should come, and another return after it. That is a claim about the future that can be scored later, which very little in this wiki can say.

**And the pattern is at maximum tension right now.** This return did not just land him in the county. It landed him in the childhood house itself — the tightest possible version of the pattern — and that house is being sold while he is standing in it.

## What would prove this wrong

1. **A relative who left and died somewhere else.** The family tree has 515 people in it. The rule has only been checked against the direct line of descent — the siblings and cousins have not been counted. If one of them left and never came back, the rule breaks. This is the most likely place for it to fail.

1. **Morley's Seattle move turning out to be a work posting** rather than a real move. He had a Selective Service record from 1945, so a military or work assignment is an ordinary possibility. If that is what it was, the only pre-Dan example disappears and the pattern shrinks to one generation.

1. **Dan leaving and ending up somewhere else.** Still open, and it should be — this is a rule about somebody who isn't finished.

## What we still don't know

- **Is this a family, or is it just the county?** Dan's mother's side turns out to be Fayette-anchored too: most of his 90 traced direct ancestors were born, lived and died within twenty miles of Uniontown, on both sides. So the pull may be regional rather than something specific to the Franks. One person can tell these apart — **Diane Van Voorhis**, Dan's maternal grandmother, who spent 35 years in Michigan with no recorded return and is still alive, so there is no ending on record yet. Nothing else in the tree separates the two explanations.

- **The cousins and siblings have not been checked.** One has: Daniel Shrum, born 1884, died 1918 at 34 in Greensburg — but he never left the region at all, so he neither confirms nor breaks anything. That is one person out of 515.

- **Nobody knows how long Morley was in Seattle.** The record has him in Brownsville in 1945, Seattle in 1957, Uniontown in 1959. So the trip home took at most two years. The blank stretch is 1946–1956.

- **Champion, Pennsylvania is the closest thing to a problem.** Morley had a Champion address in the 1996–99 phone directories, at the same time as his Hopwood one. Champion is the town at the Seven Springs ski resort, where the family rented for years before buying a unit there. Nobody has checked whether Champion is inside Fayette County. Either way he died and was buried in Hopwood, so the rule survives it.

- **There is no comparison group.** Lots of people left Fayette County in this period — the coal industry collapsed. A family that stayed put may be perfectly typical of the area rather than unusual. Until somebody compares this family against the regional average, "the family always comes back" is a description and not yet an explanation.
