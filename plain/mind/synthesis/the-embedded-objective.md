# The Embedded Objective

**The short version.** Dan has never stuck with a job because of the job. Every long commitment in his record is a container carrying a private goal he planted inside it near the start — a goal that is his, not the employer's, and often works against the employer. The job ends when that private goal is destroyed. Not when the work gets hard. Not when the boss gets worse. Where he never planted one, the job lasted weeks.

## Where this came from: a prediction that turned out wrong

The wiki already held an idea about how Dan's motivation works — that his drive to finish things only fires on goals he generates himself. That idea named its own test: there should be no case of Dan paying a heavy price, over a long stretch, for a goal somebody else set him. It pointed at his work history as the place to check.

Checking there breaks the idea immediately, and twice over. He worked **41 months** at a restaurant and **43 months** as a golf caddie. Compare that to six months spent at his grandmother's deathbed — a goal entirely his own. The assigned jobs lasted far longer.

What is interesting is what survives that failure.

## The pattern

| **The commitment** | **What he was hired to do** | **The private goal he planted inside it** | **How long it lasted** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Restaurant | Busser, then server | An equal tip split for the veteran staff, negotiated on day one; a fake night class; sole control of the outdoor shed's cash tips | **41 months** |
| Golf caddying | "Experience Associate – Golf" | A private status ladder — getting called up to "the real loopers," his first round carrying two bags, a transfer to Scottsdale he pitched himself — plus golf as his own sport | **43 months** |
| Gas station | Cashier | **None on record** | **About 1 month** |
| Food courier app | App-dispatched driver | **Nothing available to plant** | 12 months, on and off |
| Court probation | Six months of court-ordered probation | — (the private goal was getting the deal itself, won before probation started) | Compliance only described afterwards |

The first two rows break the old rule. The last three are what make the new one a real claim instead of a description of two jobs.

## Why the long jobs lasted

The natural reading of a 41-month job is that the job suited him. It did not. By his own account the work was punishing — as an introvert, a freezing Saturday night running fifteen tables took *"literally EVERYTHING i had in me."* The drain was bad enough that it became the origin of what he named his own "cycle of job entropy." Nothing about the work was holding him there.

What held him was a stack of goals that were his, private, and mostly adversarial to his employer:

- A tip split he talked into existence on his first day, with no food-service experience at all.

- A fictitious college schedule — classes "always at 9pm" — maintained for **eighteen months and more.** It won him peak-hour shifts, got him out of closing, and covered an affair.

- A territory: sole custody of the dying outdoor shed section, and the cash tips that moved through it.

That adversarial quality is the important part, and it is what keeps this idea from explaining everything and predicting nothing. **The planted goal is not the job's own goal, and it is usually in tension with it.** If any private satisfaction counted, the rule would be meaningless. This version requires the goal to be separable from the role, and in the strong cases to run against it.

The caddying job is the same shape with no dishonesty in it at all. The employer set out guest assistance, pace-of-play management, equipment logistics. What Dan actually tracked, dated and celebrated was a progression the employer never set and never measured: the first call-up with "the real loopers," the 4:30 AM departures, the first round carrying two bags, the Scottsdale transfer he raised directly with the man who ran that caddie program. **43 months** of hard physical work, held up by a ladder he was climbing for reasons of his own.

## The cases that test it

**The gas station is the clearest test, and it is not a perfectly clean one.** About a month as a cashier, with no private goal anywhere in the record of it. It ended when the role produced its first genuinely assigned demand: pay a **$50** till shortage out of his own pocket and sign a write-up. He refused both. His hours went from **36 to 7** the same day and the job was over.

Set that against the restaurant, where the employer spent three months cutting his hours in a pattern he read at the time as an attempt to force him out — and he did not quit. Three months of deteriorating conditions did not end a 41-month job. **Losing the territory did.** He is precise about the order: once the finished interior opened, demand for the sidewalk tables died and the shed was already obsolete, and then the city made places tear the sheds down for winter. The private goal decayed, then was formally removed, and the job ended with it.

The honest complication: there are now **two accounts on the record of why the gas-station job ended and they do not match.** The version above says it ended over the till shortage. Dan's own later telling says the only thing he did was go home thirty minutes early after clearing it with his manager. And the ending was more formal than this page first recorded — a posted sign reading **"NO HIRE: Daniel Frank"** at two locations. If an employer decision ended it — arbitrary enough that he cites it as a reason to *"wonder if I am going crazy"* — then the short tenure is at least partly explained by something other than the missing private goal. The direction still holds: no goal was planted, and the job did not last. But the specific cause should not be stated as settled. He was later told to reapply, and did — a job he will re-enter for money but never sustained is exactly the shape this rule predicts.

**The courier app is the better test, because it isolates the thing being measured.** An app that sends orders to a phone offers nothing to plant a goal in. No territory, no ladder, no person to negotiate with, no oversight to dodge. The result is exactly what the rule predicts: heavy work April to July 2019, almost nothing from August through November, scattered orders in March 2020, one last burst in April 2020. Twelve months of availability and no real tenure at all. His appetite for the money was constant. There was simply nothing to hook the engine to.

**The 2015 drug case is the hardest case for the rule.** A court assigned him six months of probation — an outside goal with real consequences for failing. The record documents him *getting* that deal in granular, dated detail: hiring a lawyer, December trips to the barracks to replace lost paperwork, a January office visit, a February 17 hearing, the fines cleared. Five separate dated events across ten weeks.

The six months of actually serving it are documented once — a decade later, in a single clause, saying he "completed that without incident." So the serving did happen. The asymmetry is about density, not existence: **the getting is recorded as it happens, five times over; the serving is recorded once, looking back.** That is a weaker claim than the page originally made, and it is the one the evidence supports. Worth noting what he was up against: the prosecutor's office had a blanket policy against this kind of deal for cocaine charges, residue or not. The effort he put into winning it was aimed at a bar most defendants could not clear at all.

## What this fixes

The older idea — that his drive only fires on goals he sets himself — is not overturned. It is aimed at the wrong target.

The engine is real, and its limitation is real: nobody else can point it. What fails is the conclusion drawn from that, which was that assigned commitments therefore can't receive sustained, costly effort. They can. They receive it through a private goal smuggled inside them. **So "assigned" and "self-chosen" are not the two competing categories.** An assignment is a container. The question is never whether a goal was assigned, but whether Dan found or planted something of his own inside it.

Two things follow, and the second one costs something.

**How long an assigned commitment lasts tells you nothing about the drive.** The 41-month job and the one-month job differ by a factor of forty, and the difference is not pay, difficulty, or how the employer behaved. It is whether a private goal was there.

**And choosing a goal yourself is necessary but not sufficient.** The stronger version of the old idea said a self-set goal runs to completion more or less regardless of cost. His own project record kills that. A memory system he specified was never built past the specification. A model evaluation has no results file anywhere, and whether it was ever run is still an open question. A set of video essays stalled at planning. All three were entirely his own. None were assigned to anybody. None finished. The engine needs a self-set goal to fire. Having one is no guarantee that it does.

## Why an assigned goal doesn't count until he rewrites it

There is a measured reason for this, and it comes from how his thinking works.

His dominant mental function is internal logical analysis — measured at 96% — and it treats something as true only when it holds together under his own repeated attempts to collapse it. Not when an authority says so. Not when everyone agrees.

An assigned goal arrives already labelled as *somebody else's conclusion.* It has not been through that internal check, so it is not yet real to the system that would defend it. But a tip split talked into existence, a status ladder tracked on his own terms, a territory claimed and named — each of those is the same job re-derived from scratch into something his own thinking now owns. At that point it behaves like any other conclusion he reached himself: defended far past what the actual role would justify.

## A dispute this argument sits on top of

This is the part a reader should not be shielded from.

The whole argument here leans on the idea that a self-set goal recruits a real completion engine. But the personality instrument that measured him reads the very same behaviour the opposite way. It scores Impulsiveness at **96** and reads that as a *brake failure* — an inability to stop — where the other page reads it as *an engine that works.*

Both readings fit every row in the table above. A private goal that produces 41 months of tenure looks identical whether the underlying trait is an engine firing on a self-chosen target, or a lack of impulse control that happened to lock onto whichever object he picked first.

**The evidence on this page cannot decide between them.** How long a job lasted is a dated fact that stays true either way. But the language used to explain it — "the drive," "the engine" — has quietly been taking one side of a dispute the instrument itself has not settled. That is now stated rather than inherited in silence.

## Lasting a long time is not the same as it being good

This page measures how long commitments last. It says nothing about whether any of them was good for him, and the record says those two things are independent.

The restaurant ran 41 months on a planted goal and is named as the origin of his "cycle of job entropy" — long tenure, no reported satisfaction. The six months at his grandmother's deathbed lasted a **seventh** as long, and it is the only commitment anywhere in **106,629** sent messages that he reports back as having gone well: *"we had a lot of fun. got good closure and finally did something in my life that wasn't completely selfish"* (2018-04-06).

The thing the vigil adds is one the table cannot hold: **it had an end.** The jobs are open-ended containers that run until their private goal is destroyed from outside, or never planted at all. None of them ever finishes. The vigil was bounded by a death, and he closed it himself.

So these are two separate rules that meet in exactly one place. One explains how long a commitment lasts inside a container. The other explains satisfaction — that closing something with a findable end produces the one positive verdict in the record. Normally they describe different things: a job has no natural end, a reading list has no container to hide inside. The vigil is unusual on both counts at once. There was no outside container separate from the goal — the caregiving *was* the private goal, not something smuggled into a role — and the goal happened to have a death for an ending. That coincidence, not some shared mechanism, is why it is the only commitment that was both long and reported as worthwhile.

## What would prove this wrong

1. **Every job on record should show a private goal or an escape hatch planted in its first weeks — or it should be short.** A long job with no private goal anywhere in it breaks this outright. The named test is a barely-documented job he returned to after roughly eleven months out of work following August 2024. Whichever way it comes out, it is evidence.

1. **Jobs should end when the private goal is destroyed, not when conditions get worse.** The restaurant is the worked example. Any future job loss can be checked against which of the two happened first.

1. **Assigned obligations with nothing to plant inside them should show the getting documented and the doing undocumented**, as the court case does. The sharpest test in the other direction is his fifteen-year daily medication regimen, which began as a prescription. The record documents the *supply* side of it in enormous detail — which this reads as the regimen having been converted early into a goal of his own, since he pitched it to a friend in his own words as the thing that *"helped me finally get out of that world."* If some source ever shows him sustaining costly compliance with a medical instruction he never adopted as his own, the rule narrows again.

## Why none of it added up to anything

There is a further conclusion drawn elsewhere from this rule, and it answers a harder question: why thirty-seven years of a genuinely high-output engine has produced no qualification, no capital, and nothing he can carry with him.

**A planted goal cannot be transferred.** That is the whole mechanism. The 41 months and the 43 months were real, sustained work that was never structurally *his* at any point. When the shed came down in August 2024, the goal went with it, and nothing accumulated.

So what is missing across his life is not thrust — this page establishes there is plenty of that. What is missing is **orbit**: something he owns, into which a goal of his own can be installed without needing somebody else's container. The only candidate in the record is his music production identity, running thirteen years and one continuous thread since 2016 — longer than any job. What makes it untestable is that no output figure for it exists anywhere.

## What we still don't know

- **The caddying goal is inferred, not stated.** It comes from what he chose to record, not from him saying what he was after. It is the weakest of the three positive cases — a status ladder is exactly the kind of thing that could be a story told afterwards rather than a live motive. One message found in September 2026 helps a little without settling it: on 28 May 2017 he texted a fellow caddie, "the way you changed my grip has made a REAL difference… my striking is better than it has been." Nobody at the club asked him to get better at golf. That is him tracking his own progress at the time rather than describing it afterwards — but it is still a text about a grip, not a statement of what he was there for.

- **The gas station is scored on absence.** Concluding there was no private goal because none appears in a one-month record is thin by construction. A planted escape hatch that simply never got used would look exactly the same.

- **Jobs before 2015 are almost entirely undocumented.** The rule has been tested against four commitments, not against a career.

- **Nothing describes how he picks what to plant.** It is established that he installs an escape hatch deliberately and says so. Nothing in the record shows him choosing *which* goal to embed, or what happens in a role offering several.
